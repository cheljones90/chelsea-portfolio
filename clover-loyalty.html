Overview

Clover acquired Perca to bring loyalty into its POS ecosystem, giving merchants a way to reward repeat customers without leaving the platform. The intention was right. The execution needed work. Adoption was low, setup was confusing, customers had little visibility into rewards they were earning, and a flat-rate pricing model left revenue on the table.

I was brought in as sole design lead for a 12-month engagement to reimagine the experience end-to-end, for both merchants managing their programs and customers engaging with them. The work was demoed to senior leadership at Fiserv Forum. The response was so positive they wanted to start building immediately.

On seeing the designs live in the wild

My Role

Sole design lead for the full 12-month engagement. Led competitive research and merchant interviews, planned design milestones and deliverables, designed end-to-end merchant and customer experiences across the Clover device suite, built and tested prototypes with real users, and partnered with engineering through design QA and build.

The problem.

Clover had loyalty. Merchants just weren't using it. The setup experience was unguided and time-consuming, the value of the product was never marketed clearly, and customers had no meaningful touchpoint with loyalty during checkout. Many merchants had simply given up on it.

At the same time, a flat-rate pricing model meant Clover was earning the same regardless of how deeply merchants used the product. There was no incentive structure to drive engagement, and no design system to support one. The entire experience needed to be rethought from the ground up, for merchants and customers alike.

Two distinct audiences, one coherent system. Merchants managing programs on the left, customers earning rewards on the right.

The approach.

Before designing anything, I conducted competitive analysis across seven platforms: Square, Toast, DoorDash, Lightspeed, Zoho, Par Punchh, and Loyalty Plant. I identified areas of opportunity for each competitor and mapped those opportunities across detailed user journey maps for both merchants and customers. Each journey included feature-level detail, noting which capabilities should be scoped as MVP versus future state, and which competitors were already delivering them today.

That research was then used to build a feature roadmap plan, prioritising work across sprints and quarters with clear rationale grounded in what merchants needed and what the market already proved was possible. From there the design work split across two tracks. On the merchant side: loyalty and marketing setup and onboarding, program creation, and a dashboard for viewing and managing rewards. On the customer side: signing up for loyalty, post-payment points accumulation, and checkout speed bumps to prompt sign-up before a transaction ended.

Making loyalty visible.

The customer side of the experience had a single job: make loyalty impossible to miss. Customers signing up, seeing their points accumulate after a payment, and being prompted to join before checkout ended were all moments that had to feel natural and rewarding, not interruptive. The speed bump before checkout completion was the most critical touchpoint. A well-placed, low-friction nudge that could convert a first-time customer into a loyalty member at the moment they were most engaged.

The customer journey: signing up, earning points after payment, and the checkout speed bump that turns a transaction into a loyalty moment.

The hard part.

Clover's infrastructure wasn't ready for the level of innovation the research pointed to. The blue sky ideas, grounded in best-in-class competitor patterns and real merchant needs, got scoped down significantly. That's a frustrating place to be as a designer. You've done the research. You know what users need. And you're being asked to deliver less.

The way through it was to stay focused on what advocacy actually means in practice. You don't always get to build the ideal solution. But you do get to make sure the conversations happen, the ideas are documented, and the door stays open for what comes next. Those conversations with Clover's leadership, including their CEO, planted seeds. There is now a team actively building a dedicated loyalty app and merchant marketplace, work that traces directly back to this engagement.

The experience designed across Clover's full device suite, covering merchant-facing and customer-facing screens across restaurant, service, and retail form factors.

What I learned.

Big ideas have long tails. The features that got cut from this engagement didn't disappear. They became the foundation for a follow-on team and a product direction that wouldn't have existed without the initial push. Planting a well-researched idea in the right conversation is sometimes more valuable than shipping a smaller version of it.

Customer access is worth fighting for. We interviewed merchants but couldn't access customers directly. That gap showed up in the work. The customer-side experience was designed from proxy data and competitive patterns rather than direct insight. If I ran this engagement again, I would push harder and earlier to close that research gap, even if it required creative methods to get there.

Advocacy works best when it's concrete. The same lesson from Estimates applied here. Stakeholders respond to ideas they can see and touch. When blue sky thinking got resistance, the ideas that survived were the ones mocked up in the current framework, tied back to user research, and presented with a clear rationale for why now.
